On your role as SRMC President: When and why did you decide to contest for President of SRMC? Was the experience fulfilling? What was your most memorable feat/ something that you are proud of? How much did it take a toll on your academics, or personal life?I decided to step up as President following a suggestion from Dr Dipanjan (the then DSO) when I was a member of the audit in summer 2023 after the then-President’s resignation. I took it up because I believed it wasn’t fulfilling its true potential – that of a system of self-governance where we (the student body) could make things the way we wanted them to be; and because frankly, I wanted to have good food without standing in long lines. While the SRM even in its infancy, offered better food than the private tendered mess, I found it almost agnostic to opinions from the student body (which went against its original vision), and centered solely on not letting the SRM corpus go negative. This was understandable, but I believed that if student satisfaction was ensured and numerical projections made instead of relying solely on the bank balance, good fiscal health would follow naturally. This was my aim throughout. I maintained that first, the SRMC should compute, organize and project its finances better; secondly, it should stick to its core values of inclusivity and scientific temper from which all policies should follow, and thirdly that we increase transparency and ensure rapid communication to make everyone feel like a part of SRM. The internal functioning of the council was also changed so there was active discussion on every policy (where for every idea, the presenter would have to convince the SRMC to vote on it, and we would also have to justify it to the audit); while at the same time student opinion was taken actively through GBMs, feedback forms and also routine conversations around the campus. Equally responsible were the debates with Sachin from the Audit, and Dr Dipanjan for producing the current working structure of the SRM. Through systematic efforts, consistent improvements, and transparency, we were able to grow the community’s trust in the SRMC – as a result of which we were able to turn the loss-making entity where food ran over in the first 40 minutes, to a mess where about 800 students (and faculty) were willingly subscribed – despite a pay-to-eat mess existing in parallel, and started the students’ canteen alongside. None of this would have been possible without my council members (especially Mohak – who was VP alongside me), and critique from the student community at every decision and change. After my own  experience, I now make it a point to encourage individuals who critique the system to join it and try to solve problems from within. That is fundamentally what SRM is supposed to be – a critiqueable and changeable system. And to be able to execute this vision well was extremely satisfying. For me personally, the most cherished moment was when after making all policy changes, we were able to allocate funds to subsidise meals for students who could not afford it while our net surplus remained on an uptick. The day the council approved the first batch of financial help, I remember going out with a friend who bought me a pastry to celebrate because I finally felt that my actions were helping people. Equally cherished are the individuals who have come out from successive SRMCs, who I have seen growing into responsible and broad-minded members of the student community, and the constitution we wrote together as a guide to future SRMCs.Being the President of SRMC is an extremely difficult job. Not only does the final liability for the council’s decisions come into your head, you’re equally responsible for 20 staff members and crores of student’s money. Personal attacks on people in this position do not help either. As a result, I had to grow an extremely thick skin and learn how to clearly demarcate my personal and academic boundaries. I tried my best not to compromise on my academics from the first day, and was largely successful – getting my best academic performance of all years during those (7th and 8th) semesters – despite having to miss a few lectures due to SRM work. Personal life did take a big toll however, and it took numerous rebukes from my friends before I started separating the personal from professional and started taking the Friday night off, during which I would (mostly) keep my phone down. This habit contributed immensely during my thesis work, where I was able to maintain a very healthy work-life balance.On the current batches:  Do you think the participation in campus life has gone down in the newer batches? Do you think people are more afraid to take a stance against the admin than before? When I had come to campus, I had found it to be an extremely engaging and vocal community of students and professors alike which encouraged scientific temper not just inside, but also outside the classroom. People used to engage in active and logical discussions over mailing lists which helped in having an informed and broad-minded opinion, and resulted in actually useful policy making by the SRC. I have certainly felt this culture erode gradually first with the expanding batch sizes, and then suddenly with the drastic demographic changes in MS23 and MS24. The faculty-student interaction has gone down severely with these inflated batch sizes, and Prof Gowrishankar’s overreach against faculty members vocal on social issues coupled with the curtailing of faculty email access has certainly removed professors from being a part of the larger community. The student community has also grown increasingly polarised, resulting usually in two groups of people with different outlooks and opinions who have become difficult to reconcile under a single umbrella. The SRC which is supposed to collate students’ opinions and give a direction to student resistance has unfortunately been majorly occupied with finding a “neutral” stance (with the unsaid assumption of past SRCs being too radical) for the past 2 years. In trying to not be “too radical”, they seem to have normalised accepting diktats, as passed by the administration, not understanding that the freedoms we have at IISERM, which stand at stark contrast with most campuses across the country, have been fought for by strong, vocal, and radical resistance. Thus when new anti-student regulations are proposed by the administration, the SRC has largely sought to find a middle ground, instead of rejecting compliance outright. The attitude of SRC, and especially its heads towards the administration need to thus be questioned before their election as representatives. Students need to understand that standing for these positions comes with the implicit agreement of willingness for rebellion against the administration and that these positions are not something that can be taken up to build CVs and require significant ideological clarity. I am of the opinion that we lost the fights for segregation and attendance policies the moment the cause for student opposition turned from rational reasons to blunt resistance to regulation. Without clear rational and ideological reasons in our opposition, we are nothing more than clamoring children spamming inboxes. On joining IISER: Describe why you joined IISER in the first place? Did you always want to do science? Why did you decide upon a chemistry major? And how did the interdisciplinary coursework help you (if it did, in the first place), for your current research interests?I was interested in pursuing scientific research as a career, but coming from a very small town in Haryana, I had no clue how that worked. Like most academically well-performing kids, I was poached by the engineering crowd after the 10th grade, and after a tumultuous couple of months in a coaching institute (where I felt that people focused more on rank than science), I dropped out and taught myself directly from NCERT books and had aimed for leaving the country so focused on ranks and exams after the 12th grade. Encouraged by school seniors, I had applied to a number of universities in Canada (UBC, Waterloo, York), where I had gotten in for the fall 2021 term with partial scholarship, hoping to take a break between 2020 and 2021. I had given the IAT exam only on recommendation from a former IISER-M graduate’s parent, and did not even know that biology would come in the exam until the exam hall. I joined in November 2020 on my father’s insistence that I try it out for a semester, and decided to stay just a couple of months in, because of the open culture and flexible academic environment presented to me by seniors and faculty; and the interdisciplinary course structure which would have allowed me to explore both chemistry and physics. Being selected for the INSPIRE-SHE scholarship also helped boost my decision by providing financial independence.In my first year, I had diverse interests – quantum mechanics, electrochemistry, biochemistry and computational simulation. The course structure and particularly my first two summer internships helped me narrow down my interests into experimental material sciences, which could’ve been pursued through either physics or chemistry. I chose chemistry because it had more relevant courses, and had planned to minor in Physics. However eventually I had to take a Data Science minor because of time-table clashes. While a majority of the core chemistry coursework is extremely specific and irrelevant to me (particularly organic – which was a nightmare and I barely passed with a D), others like Bala’s Numerical Chemistry, Prof Angshuman’s Electrochemistry, and especially Prof Ujjal’s Materials Chemistry and Nanosciences have contributed directly to my masters’ research. I do largely wish for the degree of flexibility in choosing courses – particularly between organic and inorganic chemistry, and I sincerely hope DCS improves diversity in its faculty recruitment and gets more material chemists.Thesis, academia and beyond: How was your thesis experience and what did you work on? comments on the increasing craze for internships abroad (which you batch kind of propagated). How much role does privilege (both social and financial) play in academia, according to you? During my thesis, I worked with analysis of electrolytes used in solid-state batteries at SSCU, IISc. Unlike typical Li-ion batteries, these do not have an easily tunable electrolyte whose composition can be changed via additives, and require careful multi-step synthesis to optimize the necessary chemo-mechanical properties. My work involved investigating the mechanics of synthesised solid electrolyte pellets by building an acoustic probe, and studying electrochemical properties and performance of cells via impedance spectroscopy (very different from electromagnetic spectroscopy). The projects were highly successful, and we are expecting some of the work to be published soon. The experience as a whole was also extremely satisfying for me as I was able to work across the spectrum – from numerical and electrochemical analysis to material synthesis and cell testing, while taking a course on advance electrochemistry to supplement the work which is mostly disconnected from what I have studied at IISER. I wouldn’t say my batch promoted foreign internships. Better quality of life, better pay (compared to none in India usually), and the thrill of travel have highly incentivised them, while also providing substantial exposure to research abroad. Some students also want to explore niche topics which just aren’t being worked on in India, or for which no positions are available. If India wants to retain its talent, I believe it must spend more on human resources than machines. However, at the same time, students must also consider that the primary purpose of internships is to get scientific exposure, and if they are compromising on good science for short term benefits in personal life, it might not work out well for their academic career. We also seem to have normalised writing 100s of emails to get these positions, notwithstanding the damage substandard work does to IISER’s image, and the spam label that our institute’s email gets associated with. Personally, it doesn’t make much sense to put in that much time and effort into applying abroad, but then I have the privilege to be able to fund a stay in Bangalore for 3 months while others might not. For masters’ thesis though, I wouldn’t discourage people from looking abroad. While my experience has been positive during my thesis at IISc with a healthy work-life balance and decent pay, I have heard too many stories of abuse at the hands of PIs in India, which are less common abroad due to strict regulations, which becomes more important for a long duration such as a masters’ thesis. I feel that privilege has a substantially larger role than it should have in academia. Communication skills and presentation, especially hold over spoken and written English play disproportionately into requirements for a successful academic career, in disseminating papers and in communicating ideas. Being a social person with the right kind of interests also factor a lot while connecting with faculty outside the classroom, which is not talked about often and plays a large role in getting the right guidance. Going into pure sciences is a significant financial decision – as unlike engineering the scope for jobs and placement is little. INSPIRE and the low fees at IISER provided some encouragement to individuals opting for this path. However the rising academic fees and the non-increment in INSPIRE scholarship have discouraged this substantially, making IISERs mostly for the financially well-off.  Plans for the future ahead? Do you wish to stay in academia? What will you miss the most about IISER?I am planning to take a break after IISER, during which I want to focus on some personal projects and finish some of the work I had started during my thesis at IISc. I am planning on starting my PhD in the starting of 2026, for which I will start applying soon. I do plan on staying in academia, but am not disinclined towards industrial research either – something to contemplate post doctorate 🙂The campus for me is really the people who made it the way it was. Some of these are faculty, but mostly, it was my batchmates and my friends – people I could rely on, chat with at any time of the day, and just ask – walk par chalna hai kya? To have such a diverse bunch of people with such like minded attitudes and passion towards their own fields who are in such close proximity – is what I’ll miss the most. Everyone’s going to different parts of the world now.
